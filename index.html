About me

I am Usman bin Affan Khan, known online as UsamUsami. I am a student and researcher of Tafsir, and a writer.

I was born in Saudi Arabia and spent my early years there, before moving to Bangladesh for my Islamic education. I am currently based in Karachi, Pakistan, where I am completing a specialisation in Tafsir and its sciences at Jamia Binoria Alamia.

My writing centres on three themes: the Qur'an, the human mind, and mental wellbeing. I work to present reliable psychological knowledge in simple language, held together with an Islamic perspective, so that readers can understand their own minds and face the pressures of everyday life with greater calm.

My writing appears regularly on my blog, and this website is the permanent home of my work.

Psychology topics

Understanding the mind, field by field

Psychology is not one subject but many, each looking at human life from a different side. Here are the fields I write and learn about, each with a short, thoughtful introduction.

Islamic Psychology

Islamic psychology looks at the human being as more than a body and a mind. It speaks of the heart, the soul and the self, and it asks how a person finds peace with Allah, with himself and with the people around him. Faith gives life a direction: when a person knows why he was created, hardship stops being meaningless and becomes a test that carries reward. The Qur'an describes the self in its states: the self that urges toward wrong, the self that reproaches itself, and the self at peace. Growth, in this view, is the journey from restlessness toward that peaceful self. Prayer, remembrance, patience and gratitude are not only acts of worship; they steady the heart, slow the racing mind and return a person to balance. Islamic psychology also guards a boundary: it encourages seeking knowledge and proper care for illness, while keeping the door of hope in Allah always open. A believer is taught neither to despair of Allah's mercy nor to ignore the means He created. When faith, self discipline and inner peace work together, a person becomes calmer in hardship, kinder in ease and steadier in purpose. That is the well being this field seeks: a heart connected to its Creator, and a life built around that connection.

Family Psychology

Family psychology studies the smallest and most important society a person ever belongs to: the home. Long before school or friends shape us, the voices, habits and affections of our family form our first picture of the world. A child who is listened to learns that his words have worth. A spouse who is treated with gentleness learns that home is a place of safety, not of fear. This field looks at how parents, children, husbands and wives affect one another's moods, confidence and behaviour every single day. It teaches that most family problems are not solved by winning arguments, but by understanding what each person is really asking for: attention, respect, rest or love. Simple habits carry great weight here: speaking softly when tired, apologising quickly, thanking openly and giving time without a screen in hand. Family psychology also reminds elders that behaviour is taught more by example than by instruction. When the home becomes a place of mercy, its members carry that mercy into the whole society. Strong families are not built in a day; they are built daily, by small acts of patience repeated with love.

Educational Psychology

Educational psychology asks a simple question: how do people actually learn? It studies attention, memory, motivation and the feelings that surround learning, so that teaching can fit the learner instead of fighting him. Every student is different: one learns by listening, another by seeing, another by doing, and a wise teacher reaches all three. This field shows that praise for effort grows a student more than praise for talent, because effort is something the student can repeat. It also explains why fear blocks learning: an anxious mind spends its strength on worry and has little left for understanding. Good method matters as much as good intention: small steps, clear examples, regular review and honest feedback turn weak beginnings into strong results. Educational psychology guides parents too, teaching them to protect a child's curiosity instead of crushing it with comparison. When learning is built on understanding, discipline and encouragement, students do not only pass examinations. They become people who love knowledge for life, and who can teach others with the same patience they were taught with.

Social Psychology

Social psychology studies how people think, feel and behave in the presence of others. None of us lives alone: our choices are shaped by family expectations, friends, crowds, customs and the quiet pressure to belong. This field explains why a person may act bravely alone yet follow the crowd against his own judgement, and why a kind word from the group can lift someone while exclusion can wound deeply. It looks at how first impressions form, how attitudes spread, and how leaders and voices in media influence whole communities. Understanding these forces protects us: we learn to pause before copying others, to check a rumour before passing it on, and to give people the same fairness we want for ourselves. Social psychology also shows the bright side of our social nature. Human beings cooperate, sacrifice and care for strangers in ways no other creature does. When we understand how groups work, we can build gatherings that encourage honesty, courage and generosity. A healthy society begins with people who know how influence works, and who choose to use their influence for good.

Health Psychology

Health psychology explores the connection between the mind and the body. It asks how our thoughts, emotions and habits affect our physical health, and how illness in the body weighs on the mind. Stress is its clearest example: a worried mind keeps the body tense, disturbs sleep, weakens appetite and slowly drains strength. On the other side, calm habits protect health in quiet ways: regular sleep, honest rest, movement, moderate food and time with people we love. This field teaches that caring for health is not only about medicine. It is also about the meanings we live by, the pace we keep, and the way we respond when life becomes heavy. Health psychology encourages small, steady changes rather than sudden, extreme ones, because the body trusts what is repeated gently. It also reminds the sick and the tired that patience is part of healing, and that a hopeful heart supports the body's recovery. When we treat the mind and body as partners, we stop neglecting one for the other. True well being is the two of them, cared for together, day after day.

Community Psychology

Community psychology looks beyond the individual and asks how neighbourhoods, villages and societies shape the health of the mind. A person does not heal or struggle in a vacuum: clean water, safe streets, fair treatment and neighbours who care all become part of his inner life. This field works on prevention before cure, building communities where problems are less likely to grow. It values cooperation over competition: people who share responsibility for their street, their school and their place of worship carry one another through hard times. Community psychology teaches that helping is not one way. The one who serves gains dignity, friendship and purpose, while the one who receives gains support, and the whole community gains trust. It pays special attention to those easily forgotten: the elderly, the poor, the newcomer and the quiet child in the corner. Small structures matter here: a study circle, a visiting habit, a shared fund for emergencies, a bench where elders are heard. When people feel they belong to a place and the place belongs to them, loneliness loses its power. Strong communities are built the same way strong families are: by people who show up for one another, again and again.

Developmental Psychology

Developmental psychology follows the human being across the whole journey of life. It studies how we grow emotionally, intellectually, socially and spiritually, from the first days of childhood to the last days of old age. Each stage has its own gifts and its own tests. The infant learns trust in a mother's arms. The child learns language, fairness and friendship through play. The young person searches for identity and purpose, asking who he is and what his life is for. The adult builds, provides and guides, while the elder gathers wisdom and learns to let go with grace. This field teaches parents and teachers to expect from each age only what that age can give, and to give each age what it truly needs. It also comforts us about change itself: difficulty in one stage is not the end of the story, because growth continues as long as life does. Understanding development makes us patient with children, respectful toward the young, and gentle with the old. And it reminds every one of us that we are still becoming, at every age, by the mercy of Allah.

Child Psychology

Child psychology is the study of the child's inner world: how children think, feel, learn and grow. A child is not a small adult. His mind understands the world in its own way, and behaviour that looks like stubbornness is often a need he cannot yet name. This field teaches that children learn first by watching. The tone of our voice, the way we handle anger and the way we treat others become their first lessons in how to be a person. Love and safety are the soil of growth: a child who feels secure explores, asks and learns, while a frightened child spends his strength on fear. Child psychology guides us to correct without shaming, to praise effort warmly, to listen to small stories as if they were great news, and to keep our promises even in tiny things. It also asks us to protect childhood itself: time to play, time to be bored, time with family away from screens. Every child carries a trust placed in our hands by Allah. To understand his mind is to guard that trust with knowledge, patience and tenderness.

Behavioral Psychology

Behavioral psychology studies what people do, and why they keep doing it. It looks at habits, actions and reactions, and at the patterns that quietly run much of our daily life. Its central insight is simple: behaviour that is rewarded tends to return, and behaviour that brings no reward slowly fades. This is why a small good habit, repeated daily, reshapes a person more than a rare burst of motivation. Behavioral psychology teaches us to work with this law instead of against it. Break a big change into a tiny first step. Attach the new habit to something you already do. Make the good action easy and the harmful one difficult, and let your surroundings help you instead of tempting you. It also helps us read others with more mercy: much of what annoys us in people is habit, not intention, and habits can change when the pattern around them changes. Parents, teachers and leaders all use this knowledge, whether they know its name or not. Above all, this field carries hope: character is not fixed at birth. By the permission of Allah, patterns can be retrained, one honest repetition at a time.

Neuropsychology

Neuropsychology studies the living bridge between the brain and behaviour. It asks how the brain supports memory, attention, language, emotion and decision, and what happens to a person's life when parts of that system are injured or unwell. Every thought we think and every feeling we feel travels through this delicate organ, which Allah created with astonishing precision. This field has shown that the brain is not fixed like stone. It changes with learning, with habit, with sleep and even with the company we keep, forming new connections across the whole of life. That is why review strengthens memory, why rest restores attention, and why repeated good actions become easier over time. Neuropsychology also brings humility. Behind a person's forgetfulness, confusion or sudden change of character there may be a struggling brain, not a bad heart, and such people deserve patience and proper care. For the student, this science deepens gratitude: to study the mind is to study one of the greatest signs of the Creator. And it encourages hope, because a brain that can change is a person who can grow, by the permission of Allah, at any age.

Listen first: understanding a person before answering

Every conversation carries a hidden order, and wisdom begins by respecting it. Before we answer a person, before we correct, advise or judge, we are meant to listen, fully and patiently, until we understand what is really being said. Much of what people say on the surface is not the whole of what they mean: behind a complaint there may be tiredness, behind anger there may be fear, and behind silence there may be a grief that has not yet found words. The one who listens first hears these deeper layers, and his answer reaches the real need instead of the outer words. Listening is not weakness or delay. It is respect made visible: it tells the other person that his heart matters more than our hurry to speak. Our Prophet, peace be upon him, listened to people completely, turning his whole attention to the one speaking, even when the words were long or difficult. Psychology confirms what good manners already knew: a person who feels heard becomes calmer, more honest and more ready to hear in return. Correction given after listening enters the heart; correction given before listening usually builds a wall. So in the family, in the classroom, in friendship and in counsel, let the first act be listening, the second understanding, and only the third, speaking. Answers are easy to give. Understanding is the gift, and it begins with a quiet, attentive ear.
